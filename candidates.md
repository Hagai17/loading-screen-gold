# Candidates — Loading Screen Gold

The bounty judge (a comedy editor for developer-tool microcopy) weighs two
tips at a time on, in order: (1) it really is a loading-screen tip for
stressed developers and AI coding agents, not a slogan, status message or
joke about something else; (2) a real laugh through a turn or punchline,
not a pun that only groans; (3) it lands in a glance, every word working;
(4) it speaks to the stressed developer and the AI coding agent at once, so
both feel the joke is about them; (5) it feels fresh, not a rerun of the
usual coffee, deadline or compile jokes. Ten drafts, duelled pairwise on
those criteria in that order.

The dual-audience criterion (4) is this brief's centre of gravity: the
winning line has to be true of the human and of the agent in the same
breath. The shared ground mined for drafts: both lose their thread when
interrupted, both run out of room (working memory / context window), both
wait on each other mid-build, and both fake confidence while stuck.

## The ten drafts

1. "Tip: If this spinner has been up five minutes, one of you is hallucinating progress. Check the model first. Then check yourself."
2. "Tip: Somewhere, your agent is waiting on your approval while you wait on its output. You've been loading each other for twenty minutes."
3. "Tip: You're both doing the same job right now — staring at a loading screen, each pretending the other one knows what they're doing."
4. "Tip: Patience is a shared resource: the developer is out of context, the agent is out of tokens, and the progress bar is out of honesty."
5. "Tip: When your agent says 'Almost done,' it learned that from you, at 6 p.m., every day this week."
6. "Tip: Restarting it won't fix the prompt. Restarting you won't fix the code. Sit with that — the bar needs another minute anyway."
7. "Tip: The agent isn't thinking. It's buffering its confidence — same as you in standup, saying the fix is 'basically done.'"
8. "Tip: You are both one 'just checking in?' away from losing the thread — a train of thought and a context window are the same size: one interruption."
9. "Tip: The build isn't slow. You and your agent are taking turns being the bottleneck, and right now it's doing the wrong thing very, very fast."
10. "Tip: 99% loaded means the last 1% holds every bug you were afraid of — the human wrote them, and the agent is about to explain them away."

## Duels

- 10 vs 9: both riff on the wait itself. 10's agent only explains bugs
  away — it is a joke about the agent told to the human, so it drops
  criterion 4; 9's "doing the wrong thing very, very fast" is a real turn,
  but "the build" drifts toward the banned compile jokes and the line
  rambles. 9 survives, wounded.
- 8 vs 7: 8 has the set's best dual concept (a train of thought and a
  context window really are the same size) but spends 150 characters
  explaining it, so it fails the glance test (3). 7's standup detail is
  concrete and dev-true, but the agent is the joke's object, not its
  co-sufferer — weaker on 4. Split decision; both fall in the next round.
- 5 vs 6: 5 is funny and specific (6 p.m., every day this week) but again
  looks at the agent from outside, and "Almost done" is close to a stock
  ETA joke. 6's mirror (prompt/code, it/you) makes both sides the subject
  and "Sit with that" is a deadpan beat a loading screen can hold. 6 wins.
- 4 vs 3: 3 is pure mutual recognition with no turn — a smile, not a
  laugh, so it loses on 2. 4's triple parallel gives each audience its own
  native vocabulary (out of context / out of tokens) and saves the turn
  for the third beat (out of honesty). 4 wins.
- 2 vs 1 (semifinal of the survivors): 2 turns the setting into the joke —
  both parties are literally mid-loading-screen, waiting on each other —
  and "loading each other" is fresh. But it is an observation, where 1 is
  shaped like advice a tip can give. On criterion 2, 1's two-step turn
  ("Check the model first. Then check yourself.") is the cleaner laugh:
  the first sentence sets the trap symmetrically ("one of you"), the last
  three words spring it on the reader. 1 wins.
- 1 vs 4 (final): 4 is the most quotable, but its list shape flirts with
  the slogan the brief warns against (criterion 1), and its laugh is a
  wry nod rather than a turn. 1 is unmistakably a tip (a condition plus an
  instruction), concrete (this spinner, five minutes), and its key word
  does double duty: "hallucinating" is the agent's signature failure,
  pinned here on whichever of the two is actually stuck — so the human
  and the agent each read it as being about the other one, then about
  themselves. 1 wins the duel and the entry.

## Winner

"Tip: If this spinner has been up five minutes, one of you is hallucinating progress. Check the model first. Then check yourself." — 128 characters.
